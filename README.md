# Lookout

A field guide to the Pacific Northwest: places to go, and the living things you'll see there. A sister site to [Myriad](https://github.com/prntwrld/myriad).

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no build step. It runs on GitHub Pages and works offline as an installable web app.

## Pages
- **Front page** (`index.html`, `app/home.js`): Today (a hike in season or a flower in bloom, the same pick for everyone all day), then **Cascadia**, a full-width map of it with your places (tap anywhere and the Atlas opens with that raindrop; a line under it says how many you've done and opens Me), where to go this month, and what you've done lately. The map is Cascadia as a bioregion: every river that reaches the Pacific from the Copper River in Alaska to Cape Mendocino in California, inland to the Continental Divide, traced from elevation data by `tools/build_cascadia.py`. It's drawn on shaded relief coloured from the theme, with the rivers and lakes in grey (or blue, with the "Rivers in blue" switch); from zoom 7 OpenStreetMap's tiles take over.
- **Places** (`places/`, `app/places.js`): the bucket list (your places are on the Atlas's map), what's in season this month, and every place, sortable and filterable. Each place (`places/?id=maple-pass`) has its numbers, which of your books pick it ("in 4 of 6 books"), a link to current conditions, a box to tick it off with a date, a note and your own photo, "You might see here" (species recorded near the trailhead this month), a map of what's around it, and the nearest places on your list. **Beyond the trail** is a map of places across Cascadia that aren't hikes (lighthouses, farmers markets, farm stands, ferries, and campgrounds, hot springs and waterfalls once Overpass has answered), from `data/seeds.json`: a chip per kind, and a Where picker for any region, sea or lake (pick Puget Sound and it lists what's on it). Tap a dot or a row to **Save** it; what you save is listed on the Me page.
- **Me** (`me/`, `app/me.js`, the Me button beside the theme switch): everything that's yours, kept on this device. **Your field guide** comes first: the 1,674 species in Lookout's keys (`data/dex.json`, written by `tools/build_dex.py`), by group, the ones you've seen in colour with when, and the rest faded, the groups you've seen most first. It fills two ways: **Seen it** on any species page (the date, how many times, and where you were if the browser shares it), and **Bring them in**, which takes an iNaturalist username and ticks off every species in that person's public sightings in Washington, Oregon, Idaho and British Columbia. Species you've seen that aren't in the keys show under **Beyond the keys**, and **Rivers you've been in** lights up the Atlas's 14 named watersheds from your places done, your sightings and your iNaturalist sightings. **Your progress** (the count and each region's bar), **Next up** (your places in season this month that you haven't done), **Near you** (`me/?f=near`), **Trips** (the list and the form to plan one), **Saved** (what you've saved from Beyond the trail, by region, with links to OpenStreetMap and the place's website), and **Your log** (what you've done, latest first, with your date, note and photo) with **Export** and **Import**.
- **Trips** (`app/trips.js`; the list and the form are on Me, and `trips/` goes there): plan a trip of up to three weeks. Give it a name, the dates and one or more regions (seas and lakes too), and its page (`trips/?id=…`) has **the days**, each with its stops in order, the miles between them as the crow flies and what that day means for each (a market's hours that day or that it's closed, the lowest daylight tide at a tide-pool spot from NOAA, a hike out of season); **the map**, with the stops numbered and joined by day and every idea a faint dot you can add from; **ideas** from inside the regions: your places in season on those dates, what you've saved, the salmon, whale and tide-pool spots in season, the farmers markets open on your days (read from OpenStreetMap's opening hours; ones with no hours listed come after), lighthouses, farm stands and ferries; and **Out in <month>**, the most-recorded species there in those months, live from iNaturalist. A place's page can add it to a trip too. Trips are kept on this device and go out with Export.
- **Atlas** (`atlas/`, `app/atlas.js`): Cascadia on one big map. **Tap anywhere and a raindrop runs downhill to the sea**, drawn along the path the water really takes, and the panel tells its story: the rivers it runs down ("the Salmon, Snake and Columbia"), where it reaches the sea, how far and from how high, the watershed it's in with a line about that river, the region, your nearest places, and what's been seen within 10 km this month (live from iNaturalist). Tap outside Cascadia and it says where the water goes instead: Hudson Bay, the Gulf of Mexico, the Arctic, or the Great Basin's closed lakes. Switches show your places, the named watersheds (Columbia, Fraser, Skeena, Klamath…), Beyond the trail, salmon, whale and tide-pool spots, borders and blue rivers; a month setting fades the places out of season and shows the salmon and whales of that month. Below, **Cascadia, a primer**: seven short chapters (what Cascadia is, the Columbia and the Fraser, the volcanoes, the rain shadow, the Ice Age floods, salmon, and the peoples of the rivers) that move the map as you read, some with a raindrop to try. A link like `atlas/#at=44.14,-114.92` opens with that raindrop. The raindrop works offline.
- **Living things** (`living/`, `app/living.js`): every plant and animal recorded in the Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon, Idaho and British Columbia), live from iNaturalist. It opens on the season: **Out in <month>** (tabs for mushrooms, blooming, migrating birds, and mammals, amphibians and reptiles, opening on the one that fits the season), **Songs & calls of the season** (Xeno-canto recordings from Wikimedia Commons and iNaturalist observers' recordings of birds, mammals and frogs heard this month, each credited with its licence, with Play all; the player is `app/sound.js`), and **Seen this week** (the most-sighted species in the last seven days). Each stays hidden when there's nothing to show, as it is offline. Below, **Explore** has a card for each group and a search for any species. Each group has a page of its own (`living/?g=birds`, `?g=mushrooms`, …): an introduction, when and where to look, how many species and sightings, its busiest months, a map of where it's recorded, its Northwest classics, what's out this month, its kinds, and its species most recorded first, 24 at a time, with a search within the group. Every group and every family, order or genus page also lists its **kinds**: the families (or genera, or orders) recorded here, most seen first, each opening its own page, so Mammals leads to Bears and Bears to the black bear. **Tide pools** (`living/?g=tidepools`) adds the month's lowest daylight tides at NOAA stations along the coast, a map of good tide-pool spots, and how to look without harm. **Near you** (`me/?f=near`: it lives on the Me page, and `living/?f=near` goes there; its code is still in `app/living.js`) asks for your location and shows, within 2, 10 or 25 km: a map of the photographs people have shared there in the last month, the latest sightings, tiles by kind (in the water, birds, trees and flowers, mushrooms…) that open to their species and the key, the best bets this month, rare finds, and your places nearby. **Salmon runs** (`living/?f=salmon`, from `data/salmon.json`) has which salmon are in the rivers and spawning this month, the six fish (chinook, sockeye, coho, chum, pink and steelhead) with their months, ten places to watch them on a map, the latest photographs from the rivers, and how to watch without harm. **Whales** (`living/?f=whales`, from `data/whales.json`) has who's around this month (orcas, humpbacks, gray whales, minke, porpoises and dolphins), a map of the last month's sightings as photo pins, ten places to watch from shore, a link to listen live to the Salish Sea's hydrophones, and the rules for watching from the water. Explore opens with these four features (Near you, Foraging, Salmon runs, Whales), then twelve groups. **Foraging and the harvest** (`living/?f=foraging`, from `data/foraging.json`) has wild food by the month (berries, greens, mushrooms, nuts and roots, the shore), each edible beside the dangerous look-alike that grows with it, the deadly plants and mushrooms, the shellfish closure maps for Washington, Oregon, Idaho and British Columbia, and the year at the market and in the garden. The season's lists are kept in the browser for the day (Seen this week for three hours), so a refresh asks nothing again. The groups (in `GROUPS` in `app/core.js`, each one or more iNaturalist taxa) are birds, mammals, plants (wildflowers, conifers, ferns, mosses and liverworts, grasses and sedges), fungi (mushrooms, lichens), insects (butterflies and moths, bees and wasps, beetles, dragonflies, flies, true bugs, grasshoppers), spiders and kin, amphibians, reptiles, fish, molluscs (slugs and snails, clams, octopus), tide pools (sea stars and urchins, anemones and jellies, crabs and barnacles, nudibranchs, chitons, sponges and sea squirts), seaweeds and kelp, worms and millipedes, and slime moulds. Species pages read like an encyclopedia entry: the name and facts on the left; the photograph, the map of where it lives and where on your list to see it on the right (on a phone, the photograph first). Each species (`living/?id=<iNaturalist number>`) has up to 12 open-licensed photographs, the months it's seen here, its classification (family and genus, opening to every main rank from kingdom down, each linking to a page of its own), its conservation status (the IUCN Red List, Canada's Species at Risk Act, the US Endangered Species Act, and NatureServe's ranking in each state or province where it's at risk), its range (whether it's native or introduced, and how often it's recorded in Washington, Oregon, Idaho and British Columbia), how many sightings there are, field marks (the description from its Wikipedia article), a tall map of where it lives beside the text (GBIF's records as hexagons, fitted to the Pacific Northwest, with your places on it; iNaturalist's grid when GBIF can't be reached), the species it's most often confused with here (from iNaturalist's identifications), its relatives in the region, and where on your list to see it (or why none of your places shows). A genus, family, order or class (`living/?id=<number>` of a higher rank) has its picture, where it sits, its status, a map, and its species recorded in the Pacific Northwest. The 300 Cascades wildflowers in `data/` (`living/?id=lupinus-latifolius`) add flowering months, elevation, colour, petals, leaves, habitat, regions and the Pojar page, and they're what the key works from.
- **The key** (`key/`, `app/key.js`): a multi-access key. It opens on "What is it?": a picture for every group, each opening its own key, and the broad groups (plants, fungi, insects, molluscs) opening a picture choice of their kinds. Every key asks questions you can answer in any order, and "Use my location" adds "Recorded near here" (within 2, 10 or 50 km, from iNaturalist's sightings around you) so a park's worth of candidates is all that's left; every choice shows how many species would be left, and the candidates show as photos. There are 27 keys: **Wildflowers** (300 Cascades species) and **Birds** (150), and from `data/packs.json` mammals, amphibians, reptiles, conifers, ferns, mosses and liverworts, grasses and sedges, mushrooms, lichens, butterflies and moths, bees, wasps, ants and galls, beetles, flies, true bugs, grasshoppers and crickets, dragonflies, spiders and kin, slugs and snails, clams and mussels, octopus and squid, tide pools (whose sub-groups open it with their kind already chosen), seaweeds and kelp, fish, worms and millipedes, and slime moulds. The month comes from today's date, and region and elevation from your location if you allow it. The keys run on data already in the page, so they work with no signal. Each also offers what's been recorded across the Pacific Northwest, a region or near you, live from iNaturalist. No AI identification anywhere.

## Your data
Everything is JSON in `data/`. These files are yours to edit by hand:

| File | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `places.json` | Your bucket list: name, kind (hike, lookout, hot-spring, ghost-town, viewpoint), region, area, trailhead lat/lon, distance, gain, high point, season (first and last month), pass, books, WTA link, and an optional Wikipedia article for the photo. **The 20 places in it are samples to replace.** Check every number against WTA before you rely on it. |
| `books.json` | Your hiking books. A place's `books` lists their ids. **The six books and each place's picks are placeholders** for you to replace with your own shelf. |
| `regions.json` | The regions of Cascadia (British Columbia, Washington, Oregon and Idaho) and their boxes, shared by places, seeds, species and the key. Each has a kind: mountains, lowlands, coast, dry (the east side) or water (the Salish Sea, Puget Sound, Hood Canal and the big lakes). Boxes may overlap, so a lighthouse can be on Vancouver Island and in the Salish Sea; where one region is wanted, the smallest land region wins. `flora: true` marks the seven Cascade regions the 300 wildflowers are chosen from. The packs built before the regions grew know only those seven, so the key offers just the regions its species are tagged with until `build_flowers.py`, `build_birds.py` and `build_pack.py` are run again (longer now: they ask iNaturalist about every land region). |
| `flower-traits.json` | The key's traits, one line per species: `colours \| petals \| leaf shape \| arrangement \| habitat`. They were written for this guide from general botanical knowledge and none of the text is copied from any field guide. Correct anything you see differently. |
| `bird-traits.json` | The bird key's traits, one line per species: `size \| colours \| bill \| habitat \| behaviour`. Also written for this guide; correct freely. |
| `traits/<key>.json` | Each other key's questions and answers: the questions (with a label for every answer), then one line per species with its answers in the same order. Add or fix lines by hand, or with `python3 tools/add_traits.py <key> "Genus species: a | b | c"`, which checks every answer against the questions. |
| `packs.json` | Which keys exist: each one's iNaturalist group, how many of its most-recorded species to include, species to leave out, and the Living-things group it keys. |
| `groups.json` | The words on each group's page: a one-line hook, an introduction, when and where to look, and its Northwest classics as scientific names. Written for Lookout; edit freely, then run `python3 tools/resolve_groups.py` to turn the names into iNaturalist numbers. |
| `foraging.json` | The Foraging page: wild foods with their harvest months and notes, each dangerous look-alike, the deadly ones, the rules, shellfish closure links, and the market's and garden's months. Written for Lookout; after editing a scientific name, run `python3 tools/resolve_names.py data/foraging.json`. |
| `salmon.json` | The Salmon runs page: the six fish with their months in the rivers and spawning, the places to watch them, and the care list. Written for Lookout; edit freely. |
| `whales.json` | The Whales page: the seven species with their best months, the shore spots, the care list and links. Written for Lookout; edit freely. |
| `tidepools.json` | The Tide pools page's spots (name, place, coordinates, a note, the nearest NOAA tide station), the stations, and the care list. |
| `seeds.json` | Written by `tools/seed_places.py`: places across Cascadia from OpenStreetMap, each with its kind, pin, region and waters, and its website, hours and Wikipedia article when OpenStreetMap has them. Don't edit it; fix the place on OpenStreetMap and seed again. |
| `pojar.json` | Page numbers in Pojar & MacKinnon, one per species, all blank to start. Fill them in and they appear on the species page as "Pojar p. 214". |

Everything else in `data/` is written by the tools, so don't edit it by hand. The page joins the hand-edited files to the built ones when it loads, so edits to traits, Pojar pages, places and books show up without a rebuild.

**Your log** (what you've done, the date, the note), what you've saved from the map and your trips are kept in the browser's local storage, and your photos are kept in its IndexedDB, both on this device only. *Me → Your log → Export* saves everything (your log, saved places, trips, the species you've seen and your iNaturalist list), photos included, as one JSON file, and *Import* merges such a file back in (on another device, or after clearing the browser).

## Building the data
The scripts in `tools/` (Python 3 with `requests` and `Pillow`) fetch from the open services and write compact JSON:

```sh
python3 tools/build_places.py    # about 10 minutes for 20 places
python3 tools/build_flowers.py   # about 35 minutes for 300 species; COUNT=150 for fewer
python3 tools/build_birds.py     # about 10 minutes for 150 birds; COUNT=200 for more
python3 tools/build_pack.py      # every key in data/packs.json, an hour or two; or name some: build_pack.py mammals ferns
python3 tools/build_dex.py        # the field guide's species list, after the packs change
python3 tools/seed_places.py     # Beyond the trail's places; or name kinds: seed_places.py lighthouse market
python3 tools/build_cascadia.py  # the map's outline, relief, rivers, borders and the Atlas's raindrop grid; about 5 minutes (needs numpy scipy shapely pillow rasterio, and numba to be quick)
```

`tools/build_index.py` (run at the end of `build_pack.py`) writes `data/packs/index.json`, the species counts and thumbnails on the "What is it?" tiles. A new key is an entry in `data/packs.json` (its iNaturalist taxa and how many species), a `data/traits/<key>.json` with its questions and one line per species, then `python3 tools/build_pack.py <key>`. Give the entry the same id as a group in `GROUPS` in `app/core.js` and the key appears under that group. The build prints any species that still need a line.

- **`build_cascadia.py`** traces Cascadia from elevation (Terrain Tiles, about 1 km): a priority flood from the sea labels where every cell's water goes. Natural Earth's rivers and lakes are cut into the ground first so the water finds the real channels, the Great Basin's closed basins are sinks, and a short list of outlets the 1 km data gets wrong is cut by hand (`CHANNELS`: the Thompson, the Babine, Takla Lake, the Pend Oreille, and the Peace through its dams). It writes the outline, the relief, the rivers and borders, and the Atlas's raindrop grid (`atlas-flow.png`, `atlas-ground.png`, `atlas.json`: which way water runs from every ~5 km block, the ground's height, and the named watersheds). The watersheds come out close to their published areas (Columbia about 662,000 km², Fraser 230,000, Skeena 54,000, Klamath 40,000).
- **`seed_places.py`** writes `data/seeds.json`. Each kind (lighthouse, market, farm, camera-obscura, ferry, campground, hot-spring, waterfall) is one Overpass query over Cascadia; when no Overpass server answers, the small kinds are found with Nominatim's category search tile by tile at one request a second, and the big ones (campgrounds, hot springs, waterfalls) keep what's already seeded until Overpass is back. It keeps named places inside `data/cascadia.json`'s outline, drops navigation lights tagged as lighthouses, and merges duplicates. Naming kinds reseeds just those. `NO_OVERPASS=1` skips Overpass.
- **`build_places.py`** writes `data/places-extra.json`. It finds the trailhead pin and named peaks, lookouts, hot springs, viewpoints, lakes and waterfalls nearby using OpenStreetMap (Overpass, or Nominatim when no Overpass server answers). It takes a photo and two sentences from Wikipedia (freely licensed photos only, with author and licence), and records what iNaturalist observers have seen within 3 km in each month.
- **`build_flowers.py`** writes `data/flowers.json` and `data/thumbs/`. It picks the most-recorded native wildflowers across the Cascade regions (`tools/flora_rules.py` leaves out grasses, sedges, shrubs, trees and pond weeds) and fetches each one's names, family and the Wikipedia summary iNaturalist carries. Photos are Creative Commons only (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA; never "no derivatives" or all rights reserved), each credited to its photographer. iNaturalist's own picks for the species come first, then Wikipedia's lead picture, then the region's sightings, with grey, dark or flat pictures moved back. Flowering months come from sightings annotated as flowering, regions from where it has been recorded, and elevation from the heights of its sightings (Open Topo Data, SRTM 30 m). It also saves a 200 px thumbnail of each species in the repository, so the key and the guide work offline.

Every request is paced (about one a second for iNaturalist, Nominatim and Open Topo Data, as each asks) and cached in `tools/.cache/`, so a rerun only asks for what's new. Set `PAUSE_INATURALIST=2` (or `PAUSE_NOMINATIM`, `PAUSE_OPENTOPODATA`) to go slower.

## Live and saved
Living things, species pages and "You might see here" ask iNaturalist as you browse (photos follow the same Creative Commons rule as the packs; non-commercial licences are used because Lookout is a non-commercial site, so revisit that if it ever becomes commercial). The wildflower pack, the places and your log are files in the repository and on your device.

## Offline
`sw.js` is the service worker. On install it saves the app shell, the data packs and every wildflower thumbnail. Pages, scripts and data load fresh when there's a connection, so your edits show up, and come from the saved copy only when there's no connection. Photos and map tiles are saved as you view them, so a place or species you've opened before still has its pictures offline. When you change the list of shell files in `sw.js`, bump `VERSION` there.

## Later, as an iOS app
The site is built so that a native app can wrap it or read the same data without a rewrite:
- **Capacitor:** every path is relative and there's no server code, so the repository root can be the `webDir` as it is. Everything that touches the device goes through three objects in `app/core.js`: `Lookout.data` (the JSON packs), `Lookout.store` (settings and your log) and `Lookout.photos` (your photos). Swap those for Capacitor's Filesystem and Preferences plugins, and use Geolocation for the key, if you want native storage.
- **SwiftUI:** the JSON in `data/` is the app's data model. `places.json` and `places-extra.json` are joined by `id`, and `flowers.json` and `flower-traits.json` by scientific name. The key's matching rules are the `fits()` function in `app/key.js`. The log export format (`{log, photos}`) is the same on both sides.

## Testing
`tools/check_site.mjs` uses the pre-installed Playwright. It loads every kind of page at 1366, 880 and 390 px wide and checks for sideways scrolling, wrapped labels, empty sections and script errors. It then loads the site, cuts the network, and checks that the key and the pages you've already opened still work.

```sh
node tools/check_site.mjs        # screenshots go to $SHOTS (default: /tmp/lookout-shots)
```
